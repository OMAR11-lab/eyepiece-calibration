Drag to rotate the model.
  • Eyepiece graticule: a ruler etched on a glass disc inside the eyepiece. It is fixed, with 100 units.
  • Objective lens: on the revolving nosepiece. Turning to a different lens changes the magnification, so the graticule must be calibrated again.
  • Stage micrometer: a slide with a scale of known length, placed on the stage. It is replaced by the specimen slide when measuring.

Calibration formula

1 graticule unit (µm) = stage micrometer length (µm) ÷ graticule units it spans

Key points

  • Low power: 100 µm looks small, so many graticule units span it. Each unit is a larger real distance.
  • High power: 100 µm looks large, so fewer graticule units span it. Each unit is a smaller real distance.
  • The specimen never changes size. Only the number of graticule units it covers changes.
  • Calibrate again every time you change the objective lens.
  • Real length = graticule units counted × calibration for that objective.

In this model each micrometer division is 10 µm. In an exam the question tells you the value of each division (for example 0.1 mm), so use the value you are given. The method is identical. In real practice the micrometer slide is swapped for the specimen slide. Both are drawn together here for illustration. Light microscope vs electron microscope: resolution

Resolution is the smallest distance between two points that can still be seen as separate. Anything smaller than this cannot be seen, however much the image is enlarged. The light microscope (LM) resolves 200 nm. The electron microscope (EM) resolves 0.5 nm.

How to read this: compare the size of the particular feature (not just the whole cell) with the resolution limit. A bacterium is large enough to see with an LM, but its much smaller ribosomes are not. A mitochondrion can be seen with an LM, but its cristae cannot be resolved.

Quick check

Key points

  • Resolution is not the same as magnification. Enlarging an LM image further makes it bigger and blurrier but shows no new detail.
  • The LM limit is about 200 nm because light has a long wavelength. Electrons have a much shorter wavelength, so the EM can resolve about 0.5 nm.
  • Many viruses (for example, a 100 nm virus), ribosomes, cell membranes and cristae details are smaller than 200 nm, so they cannot be resolved with a conventional light microscope.
  • Cells, nuclei and mitochondria (as tiny rods) are larger than 200 nm, so they can be seen with a light microscope.
  • LM images are in colour (after staining) and can show living specimens. EM images are black and white and the specimen is dead and in a vacuum.

Sizes are typical approximate examples; viruses vary widely in size. Cristae are folds of the inner mitochondrial membrane, and the value shown is an approximate feature dimension, not the length of a crista.
